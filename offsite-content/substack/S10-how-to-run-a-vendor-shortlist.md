# Issue 10: How to run a vendor shortlist

Last issue. Thank you for reading, and particularly to the people who replied
with disagreements — three of those changed what I wrote in issues four and
seven.

I have saved the thing I get asked about most, and about which I have the least
systematic view. How do you choose a vendor — an agency, a development firm, a
platform — when every one of them is good at being chosen?

Three parts: why the published comparisons do not help, why the pitch selects for
the wrong qualities, and the six questions that do most of the work.

## Part one: the lists are directories, written in the register of assessments

Search for the best anything-companies in any market and you will get dozens of
near-identical pages. Ten to fifteen firms, a paragraph each, a "what to look
for" section, an FAQ.

I should disclose that my own employer publishes pages of exactly this kind,
[including ones my own colleagues publish](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/best-seo-agencies-australia)
for the Australian market. Everything below applies to those too.

They are written from public information — mostly the firms' own marketing
material. They have not involved talking to those firms' clients. They exist in
part because people search for this, and that is a reasonable way to be found.

The reason they all look the same is economic rather than lazy:

**Genuinely assessing fifteen firms** means reading their code, talking to their
clients, understanding their delivery process. Weeks of expert time, not
recoverable from the page, and no reader can tell from the page whether you did
it. So nobody does.

**The source material is identical.** Every firm describes itself as having
senior engineers, transparent communication and a client-first approach.
Summarise fifteen of those and you get fifteen indistinguishable paragraphs,
because the input was fifteen indistinguishable positioning statements.

**Everyone reads each other's lists.** The fastest way to assemble candidates is
to read existing lists, which produces convergence on the same names, which
makes those names look more credible, which gets them onto more lists.

That last mechanism is the important one: **list frequency measures marketing
persistence, not quality.** Firms that appear everywhere have been doing content
and outreach for five years. That is genuinely informative — they are
established and reasonably professional — but it is not what the page claims to
measure.

So: use these pages for **candidate discovery only.** Read six, collect every
name, ignore the ordering entirely. That is real value and it is the only value.

## Part two: the pitch selects for confidence

The standard pitch is: an audit with a number of problems found, a gap analysis
showing you behind competitors, case studies with percentage improvements, and a
proposal with a retainer and deliverables.

Every element establishes that there is a problem and that they have a process.
None establishes that their process caused the results in their case studies,
which is the only thing you are buying.

And the format has converged because it works on buyers. Which means the
selection process rewards the firms best at pitching, and pitching skill is
roughly uncorrelated with delivery skill.

Good work in most of these fields is unglamorous. It consists of doing sensible
things patiently, and the people who do it tend to present *less* impressively,
because their story has fewer numbers and more caveats. The structural problem is
that the pitch rewards confidence and the work rewards patience.

## Part three: the six questions

These are the ones that produce genuinely different answers from good and bad
vendors. I have used them on agencies, development firms and platform vendors,
and they transfer.

**1. "What were the absolute numbers, before and after?"**

Case studies are quoted in percentages because percentages are chosen to be
large. 340% growth from 200 to 880 is real on a tiny baseline and tells you
nothing about a mature system.

Ask for absolute figures, the time period, and the starting position. Then ask
the follow-up that separates asset-builders from rank-renters: *what happened in
the twelve months after the engagement ended?*

**2. "Which of those results would have happened anyway?"**

This produces the most interesting silence of the six.

Outcomes improve for lots of reasons: the market grew, a competitor exited,
there was a PR event, a product launched, paid spend increased. A good vendor has
thought hard about attribution, because they want credit for the right things —
they will segment, reference a baseline, and separate branded from non-branded
effects unprompted.

A vendor who has never considered the counterfactual is either not analytical or
relying on you not to ask.

**3. "What will you do in month one that is not an audit?"**

Audits are the vendor's cheapest deliverable and your least valuable one. A tool
produces one in twenty minutes.

This question tests whether they have a thesis about *your* situation. A good
answer sounds like a hypothesis with a mechanism: "your category pages compete
with your blog for the same terms and the blog wins, which is why the commercial
pages don't convert — we'd fix that first." A bad answer is a list of
deliverables.

**4. "What is the binding constraint, and is it something you can fix?"**

The question underneath every engagement. Frequently the real constraint is
something the vendor cannot touch: a six-week engineering queue, a product that
does not convert, a market dominated by three incumbents you will not outrank.

A good vendor names it, including when naming it costs them the sale. I have had
one tell me "you have a six-week dev queue; recommendations you cannot implement
are not worth paying for, fix that first" — and that firm got the work eighteen
months later because I remembered.

**5. "What would make you tell me to stop paying you?"**

The same question from the other side, and it tests whether they have located
the boundary of their own usefulness. A vendor with no answer will keep
invoicing past it, not from malice but because nobody has defined where "done"
is.

**6. "Who specifically does the work, how many other accounts do they carry, and
can I speak to them before signing?"**

The person pitching is often not the person delivering. Fine if disclosed.

Then do the arithmetic out loud: divide the retainer by a plausible blended rate
and see how many hours you are buying. Twelve accounts per person means roughly
three hours a month on yours. The gap between that number and the promised
deliverables is the honest measure of the proposal.

## The thing underneath all six

Each asks the same question in a different register: **is there a causal story,
and can you show me the mechanism?**

That is the only reliable signal I have found, and it is reliable because it is
hard to fake under follow-up questions. Confidence is easy to perform. A causal
story that survives three "why" questions is not.

## What I would actually build instead

If anyone wants to make something better than the comparison lists, the gap is
clear, and it is not a content gap.

The useful artefact is not a ranking. It is a comparison on dimensions a buyer
can act on: team size, the actual location of the delivery team rather than the
sales office, named technologies with evidence of depth, typical engagement
size, minimum contract, whether they will work inside your codebase or only on
discrete projects, and who you would report to.

None of that is in any list I have read, including the ones my colleagues write,
because the firms do not publish it and collecting it means asking fifteen
companies fifteen questions and getting answers from perhaps six.

Which is the real reason these pages all look the same: the information that
would differentiate them is information nobody has, and the information everybody
has is marketing copy.

And frequently the answer to the whole exercise is that you do not need a vendor
at all — you need two engineering sprints and someone with the authority to
decide what the system is for. That build-versus-buy-versus-hire question is one
my colleagues wrote up as
[in-house versus outsourced](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/how-to-choose-software-development-company),
and it is worth settling before you start reading comparison pages, because the
pages assume the answer.

## Closing the series

Ten issues on one claim: **almost every expensive technology surprise is a
structural decision showing up late.**

Migration costs that were a wave-ordering decision. Cloud bills that were a
service-boundary decision. Rewrites that were a working-conditions problem
mislabelled as an architecture problem. Agent projects that were workflow
projects. RAG systems that were content projects. Augmentation failures that were
contract failures. Mobile costs that were an OS-support-policy decision. Security
programmes that were a control-selection decision.

In every case the information was available at the time, and in every case
nobody was looking, because the question had been assigned to a different
function.

I do not have a clean solution to that, and I distrust anyone who claims one. The
best I have is the habit: when something costs more than expected, do not ask
what went wrong. Ask **which decision, made earlier, in a different department,
is now arriving.** The answer is usually findable, and it is usually not where
the post-mortem is looking.

Thank you for reading. If I start a second series it will be about the gap
between what an architecture diagram asserts and what the system does, which is
a subject I have been avoiding because I do not yet know what I think.
