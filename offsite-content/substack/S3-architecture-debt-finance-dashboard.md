# Issue 3: Architecture debt shows up on the finance dashboard first

Here is a pattern I have now seen four times, closely enough to be confident
about the mechanism.

A company's cloud bill is uncomfortable. Somebody runs a cost-optimisation
programme. Rightsizing, reserved capacity, deleting orphaned volumes, turning
non-production environments off overnight, negotiating a committed-spend
discount.

It works. The bill comes down 20-25% in a quarter. There is a slide.

Then over the following two or three quarters it climbs back to roughly where it
started, and the second cost-optimisation programme gets a worse reception than
the first.

## Why the second quarter undoes the first

Because the first programme fixed consequences, and the causes were untouched.

The things a cost programme typically fixes — oversized instances, idle
resources, un-discounted on-demand usage, dev environments running at 3am — are
all *symptoms of a structure that keeps producing them*. Delete the orphaned
volumes and the process that orphans volumes is still running. Rightsize the
instances and the deployment pattern that oversizes them is unchanged.

Meanwhile the actual cost drivers are things like:

- A messaging cluster replicating across availability zones on every write,
  paying cross-zone charges in both directions, forever.
- A reporting service pulling full table extracts from another service's
  database because there is no event stream, so the same data moves nightly at
  full volume.
- A batch pipeline re-reading the same reference dataset forty times in a single
  run, because each step was written independently and nobody owns the pipeline.
- A service boundary drawn around team ownership rather than data locality, so a
  request path crosses the network four times where it could cross once.

Every one of those is a recurring charge generated by a design decision. Pricing
levers reduce the *unit cost* of running them. They do not stop you running
them.

So the saving is real and bounded, and the growth underneath it continues. Hence
one good quarter.

## The finance dashboard as an architecture readout

What I find genuinely interesting about this is the direction of information
flow. The finance dashboard is often the **first** place architectural
incoherence becomes visible, and it shows up there before it shows up in any
engineering metric.

Think about why. A chatty service boundary does not necessarily make latency bad
enough to alert on. The nightly full-table extract completes within its window.
The over-replicated cluster is perfectly healthy. Nothing is broken. Every
dashboard an engineering team looks at is green.

But each of those is converting a design decision into a monthly charge, so the
bill grows in a shape that matches the architecture. The line item called "data
transfer" is a measurement of how much your services talk across boundaries.
The compute line is a measurement of your scaling model. These are
architectural facts, denominated in dollars.

Which means — and this is the part I want to land — **the cost report is
observability.** It is lagging, badly labelled, and aggregated by the wrong
dimensions, but it contains information about structure that no engineering
dashboard contains.

My colleagues made this argument at more length, calling cloud cost
[an architecture problem wearing a finance costume](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/cloud-cost-is-an-architecture-problem),
which is a better phrase than any I have come up with and matches what I have
watched happen.

## The gap that makes this hard

I do not want to present this as a process failure with a process fix, because
the real obstacle is organisational and it is genuinely difficult.

The people who can read the cost shape of an architecture are architects and
senior engineers. They are fully occupied with delivery.

The people who own the cost conversation are in finance. They cannot read an
architecture, and no dashboard fixes that — giving finance a more detailed
breakdown of services they do not understand produces more precise confusion.

The gap between those two groups is where this money goes. And both groups are
behaving reasonably: finance is asking the right question in the only vocabulary
it has, and engineering is answering a different question because that is the
one it was asked.

What closed it on one programme I was part of — eventually, after about two years
of not closing it — was putting one engineer in the monthly finance review with a
standing agenda item. Three slides, every month:

1. The three largest cost drivers this month.
2. The design decision behind each.
3. What changing it would cost in engineering time.

That is it. No dashboard, no tooling, no FinOps platform. One engineer, three
slides, once a month.

That meeting is where the batch pipeline got its cache and the reporting service
got its event stream. Both had been obvious to engineers for over a year. Neither
had ever been prioritised, because they were invisible to the people doing the
prioritising — there was no feature in them, no incident driving them, and the
cost case had never been made in a room where it counted.

It should have existed in month one. I think it is probably the highest-leverage
meeting in any organisation spending meaningfully on cloud, and it costs an hour.

## The question that actually works

If you take one thing from this issue, take the reframing of the question.

The standard cost question is: **"can we reduce this line item?"** It leads to
pricing levers, because that is what it is asking about.

The better question is: **"why does this line item exist?"**

Applied to the four examples above, that question produces:

- *Why do we pay cross-zone transfer on every write?* Because the cluster is
  configured for an availability target nobody has validated against the
  business requirement. Maybe it is correct. Maybe it was a default.
- *Why does the reporting service read the whole table nightly?* Because there is
  no change stream. One is a week of work and removes the charge permanently.
- *Why does the pipeline read the reference data forty times?* Because nobody
  owns the pipeline end to end. A cache and an owner.
- *Why does this request cross the network four times?* Because the service
  boundaries follow the org chart from two reorganisations ago.

None of those answers is "we need a better discount". Three of the four are a
week or two of engineering with a permanent effect, which is a much better
return than a commitment discount that locks you into the current structure for
three years.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is the trap: **a committed-spend
discount on a bad structure is worse than no discount.** You have reduced the
unit price and simultaneously made it harder to justify changing the thing,
because now you have committed capacity to fill.

## What I would do with one afternoon

Take last month's bill. Sort by cost, descending. Take the top five lines.

For each one, write a single sentence explaining which design decision produces
it. Not which service — which *decision*.

If you can write that sentence for all five, you are in better shape than most
organisations I have seen, and you have a prioritised architecture backlog with
dollar figures attached, which is the most fundable engineering artefact there
is.

If you cannot write it for three of them, that is the actual finding, and it is
usually an attribution problem rather than an understanding problem — which is
issue two's third number coming back round.
