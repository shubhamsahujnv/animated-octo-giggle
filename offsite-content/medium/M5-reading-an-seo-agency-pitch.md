# How to read an SEO agency pitch without getting sold

I have been in the room for maybe thirty of these, on both sides. The pitches
are remarkably similar, which is itself informative: they have converged on a
format that works on buyers, and that format is largely decoupled from whether
the agency is any good.

This is not a post about which agencies to hire. It is about the six questions
that produce different answers from good and bad agencies, and why the standard
pitch is constructed to avoid them.

## First, what the standard pitch contains

You will almost always get, in this order: a site audit with a number of
"errors" found, a list of keywords you are not ranking for with their search
volumes, a competitor comparison showing you behind, some case studies with
percentage increases, and a proposal with a monthly retainer and a list of
deliverables.

Every element of that is designed to establish that there is a problem and that
they have a process. None of it establishes that their process caused the
results in their case studies, which is the only thing you are actually buying.

So: six questions.

## 1. "What was this client's traffic before you started, in absolute numbers?"

Case studies are quoted in percentages. Percentages are chosen because they are
large.

"We grew organic traffic 340%" from 200 visits a month to 880 is a real
achievement on a tiny site and tells you almost nothing about what they could do
for a site with 40,000 visits. The mechanisms that take you from 200 to 880 —
fixing indexation, writing the first twenty pages, claiming the brand term — do
not exist on a mature site.

Ask for absolute before-and-after, the time period, and the starting domain
authority. A good agency gives you all three without flinching. An agency that
only has percentages is telling you their wins were on small sites, or that the
baseline was unflattering.

Follow-up worth asking: *what happened to that client's traffic in the twelve
months after the engagement ended?* The answer separates agencies who build
assets from agencies who rent you rankings.

## 2. "Which of those results would have happened anyway?"

This is the question that produces the most interesting silence.

Organic traffic grows for lots of reasons: the market grew, a competitor exited,
a Google update happened to favour the site's shape, seasonality, a PR event, a
product launch, the brand got better known through paid channels.

A good SEO agency has thought carefully about attribution, because they want
credit for the right things. They will talk about non-brand traffic separately
from brand, segment by landing page, and reference a baseline or a control.

An agency that has never considered the counterfactual is either not analytical
or is relying on you not asking. Both are disqualifying.

A specific probe: *do you report brand and non-brand organic separately?* If
they do not, their headline number includes people typing the company name,
which SEO did not cause.

## 3. "What will you do in month one that is not an audit?"

Audits are the agency's cheapest deliverable and your least valuable one. A
crawler produces an audit in twenty minutes; the consultant writes a summary.
Most of the "errors" are low-impact — missing alt text, meta descriptions a few
characters too long, a handful of redirect chains.

That work is not useless, but it is rarely why a site does not rank. It is
popular because it is schedulable, demonstrable, and requires no judgement.

The question tests whether they have a thesis about *your* site. A good answer
sounds like: "your category pages are competing with your blog posts for the
same terms, and the blog posts are winning, which is why the commercial pages do
not convert — we would fix that information architecture first." That is a
hypothesis with a mechanism.

A bad answer is a list of deliverables.

## 4. "How many links will you build, from where, and would you show me the last ten?"

Authority is usually the binding constraint — in my experience most sites that
are stuck are stuck on authority, not on-page work — so this is the most
important question and the one with the most evasive answers.

The honest spectrum:

- **"We do digital PR; links are earned and we cannot guarantee numbers."** This
  is the credible answer. It is also slow and expensive, and they should say so.
- **"We have relationships with publishers in your sector."** Plausible. Ask to
  see examples and check whether those sites have genuine audiences or exist to
  host paid placements.
- **"We'll build 40 links a month."** Nobody earns 40 editorial links a month.
  These are bought, from a network, and the risk sits with you.
- **"Links are not that important any more."** This is usually said by agencies
  that cannot build them.

Then ask for the last ten links they built for any client. Look at the pages
they are on. Does the page have other content? Does the site have real traffic?
Is your link one of nine outbound commercial links in a 600-word post about
nothing?

The reason this matters more than the rest combined: on-page work has a ceiling
set by your authority. If your domain has a few dozen referring domains and the
page-one incumbents have hundreds, no amount of content optimisation closes
that. An agency selling you content when your constraint is authority is
selling you the thing they can deliver rather than the thing you need.

## 5. "What would make you tell me to stop paying you?"

A real consultant has a view on when their work stops being worth it. Answers I
have heard and respected:

- "If your product pages do not convert, more traffic is a waste — fix that
  first."
- "Your category is dominated by three marketplaces; you will not outrank them,
  and you would do better on the long tail or on a different channel."
- "You have a six-week dev queue. SEO recommendations that cannot be implemented
  are not worth paying for."

That last one is the single most common real blocker and almost no agency raises
it in a pitch. SEO is largely a series of changes to a website; if your
engineering team cannot ship them, the retainer buys you a backlog.

An agency with no answer to this question has not located the boundary of their
own usefulness, which means they will keep invoicing past it.

## 6. "Who, specifically, will do the work?"

The person pitching is frequently not the person delivering. Normal, and fine if
disclosed.

Ask for the named individual, their experience, how many other accounts they
carry, and whether any of it is subcontracted. Then ask to speak to them before
signing.

Twelve accounts per strategist means roughly three hours a month on yours. That
can be fine for maintenance and is not enough to change anything. The arithmetic
is worth doing out loud: divide the retainer by a plausible blended rate and see
how many hours you are actually buying. The gap between that number and the
promised deliverables is the honest measure of the proposal.

## The pattern underneath all six

Each question asks the same thing in a different register: **is there a causal
story, and can you show me the mechanism?**

Good SEO is unglamorous and mostly consists of: making the site crawlable and
fast, organising information so that one page answers one intent, building
genuine authority slowly through work that is hard to fake, and removing
whatever is preventing the engineering team from shipping changes. Agencies that
do this are usually less impressive in a pitch than agencies that do not,
because their story has fewer numbers in it and more caveats.

The structural problem is that the pitch rewards confidence and the work rewards
patience, so the selection process is biased against the thing you want.

## A note on how the market looks from the inside

One more thing, since it is relevant to anyone reading agency comparison lists.

Search "best SEO agencies" plus any country and you will find dozens of listicle
pages, most of them published by agencies, directories, or content sites with no
assessment process. The ordering is frequently commercial. They cite each other.

That is not a reason to ignore them entirely — they are a reasonable way to
assemble a longlist of names you had not heard of. It is a reason not to treat
the ordering as a ranking. The useful move is to take the names and apply the
six questions, which is work the list has not done for you.

If you are shortlisting in Australia specifically, a survey of
[the Australian agency landscape](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/best-seo-agencies-australia)
is a reasonable place to collect names across Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and
Perth before you start asking questions. And if what you actually need turns out
to be implementation capacity rather than strategy — which question 5 frequently
reveals —
[Australian web development firms](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/top-web-development-companies-australia)
may be the more useful shortlist, because an SEO retainer on top of a blocked
dev queue is money for recommendations nobody will implement.

That is, genuinely, the most common outcome I have seen: the company did not
need an SEO agency. It needed two engineering sprints and someone with authority
to decide what the category pages are for.
