# Why every "top 10 companies" list looks the same

Search for the best software development companies in any country and you will
get dozens of near-identical pages. Ten to fifteen firms, a paragraph each, a
"what to look for when choosing" section, an FAQ. Different sites, overlapping
firms, same structure, same adjectives.

I should disclose up front that I have a stake in this: I have been listed on
these pages, I have paid to be listed on one, and I have written one. So treat
what follows as an insider's account rather than a disinterested one.

The sameness is not laziness. It is the predictable output of the economics, and
understanding the economics tells you exactly which parts of these pages to
trust.

## Four kinds of page, which look identical

**The directory.** Clutch, GoodFirms and similar. Their business is listings;
firms claim profiles, collect verified client reviews, and pay for placement in
sponsored positions. The reviews are often genuinely verified, which makes these
the most useful of the four — but the *ordering* is a mix of review score,
activity and spend.

**The lead-gen site.** Exists to rank for "best X companies", collect enquiries,
and sell them. Listed firms frequently pay per listing or per lead. The
assessment is whatever the writer did in an afternoon. These are the majority of
results for most queries.

**The competitor's content marketing.** A firm writes "the best X companies" and
lists itself first or second, with nine plausible competitors around it. The nine
are chosen partly for credibility and partly for not being a direct threat in
the author's own market.

**The occasional real review.** A journalist or analyst with a methodology. Rare,
and usually behind a paywall or published by an analyst firm.

From the outside, all four render as a page with ten names on it, and nothing in
the page's design tells you which you are reading.

## Why the content converges

Four forces, each individually reasonable:

**The query is the brief.** Someone searching "best mobile app development
companies" wants a list. The page that ranks is the page that most directly
provides a list. Every publisher is optimising against the same query, so every
page converges on the same shape. There is no competitive advantage in being
structurally different, and a real penalty for it.

**The research cost is high and the differentiation value is low.** Genuinely
assessing fifteen development firms means reading code, talking to their
clients, understanding their delivery process — weeks of expert time. It is not
recoverable from the page's revenue, and no reader can tell from the page
whether you did it. So nobody does.

**The source material is the firms' own marketing.** Every entry is written from
the company's website and case studies. They all describe themselves as
having senior engineers, transparent communication and a client-first approach.
Summarise fifteen of those and you get fifteen indistinguishable paragraphs,
because the input was fifteen indistinguishable positioning statements.

**Everyone reads each other's lists.** When compiling one, the fastest way to
assemble candidates is to read existing lists. This produces convergence on the
same names, which then appear on more lists, which makes them appear more
credible. A firm's presence on these lists is substantially a function of having
previously been on these lists.

That last mechanism is the one worth internalising, because it means **list
frequency measures marketing persistence, not quality.** The firms that appear
everywhere are the firms that have been doing content and outreach for five
years. That is genuinely informative — it means they are established and
reasonably professional — but it is not what the page claims to be telling you.

## What these pages are actually good for

Not nothing. Two things:

**Candidate discovery.** You will find names you had not heard of. That is real
value, and it is the only value. Read six lists, collect every name, and you have
a reasonable longlist of firms that exist and are active in your market.

**Negative signal.** A firm that appears on *no* list in a market where lists are
plentiful is either very new, very small, or not doing any outbound marketing.
Any of those might be fine — some of the best boutiques do no marketing at all —
but it is worth asking why.

What they are not good for: **ordering, assessment, or fit.** Position one is not
better than position seven. The adjectives are from the firm's own website. And
the question you actually have — can this specific firm do this specific thing
for my specific constraints — is not addressable by a page that has never met
you.

## How to read one properly

Five checks that take about ten minutes:

**1. Find the money.** Look for "sponsored", "partner", "advertiser disclosure",
affiliate parameters in the outbound links, or a "get listed" page in the
navigation. A "get listed" link means the list is a product. That does not make
it worthless — it makes the ordering commercial.

**2. Check who published it.** If it is a firm in the same industry, the list is
content marketing and the ordering reflects the author's interests. This includes
lists where the author does not appear: they may be positioning adjacent to the
names, or the page may exist to rank and capture enquiries.

**3. Look for a methodology, and then look for evidence of it.** Many pages
claim one. Few show their work. "We evaluated on experience, portfolio and client
feedback" with no indication of what was found is a sentence, not a method. What
would convince me: a named data source, a count of reviews examined, a stated
exclusion.

**4. Check the dates.** Pages titled "2026" are frequently 2022 pages with the
year edited. Look at whether any entry references anything recent. A list that
has not been genuinely revisited is describing a market that has moved.

**5. Ignore the order entirely and use the names.** This is the main practical
advice. Treat the page as an unordered set of candidates, then do your own
assessment.

## The honest disclosure

I said I would be specific about my own interest, so: the firm I am associated
with publishes pages of exactly this kind. There is a set of them on
[Pixorama's blog](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog), including
[their Australian SEO roundup](https://pixoramagroup.com/blog/best-seo-agencies-australia),
and everything in this post applies to those too.

They are written from public information, including the firms' own marketing.
They have not involved talking to those firms' clients. They exist in part
because people search for this and it is a reasonable way to be found. If you
read one, use it for the names and apply the five checks, same as any other.

I do not think that is cynical. The pages are genuinely useful for candidate
discovery and I would rather say what they are than pretend to a methodology
nobody applies. The failure in this genre is not that the pages exist; it is that
they are written in the register of an assessment when they are a directory.

## What would actually be useful

If anyone wants to build a better version, the gap is clear and it is not a
content gap.

The useful artefact is not a ranked list. It is a **comparison on dimensions a
buyer can act on**: team size, actual location of the delivery team (not the
sales office), named technologies with depth evidence, typical engagement size,
minimum contract, whether they will work inside your codebase or only on
projects, and who you would be reporting to.

None of that is in any list I have read, including the ones I have written,
because the firms do not publish it and collecting it means asking fifteen
companies fifteen questions and getting answers from maybe six.

Which is the actual reason every one of these pages looks the same: the
information that would differentiate them is information nobody has, and the
information everybody has is the firms' own marketing copy.

So read six, take the names, and go and ask the questions yourself. That part
has never been outsourceable.
