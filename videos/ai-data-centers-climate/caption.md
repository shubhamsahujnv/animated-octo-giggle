## LinkedIn

AI's growth is now a climate variable, not a footnote.

→ US data centres used ~4% of US electricity in 2024 (183 TWh). Projections for 2030 range from 9% to 12%.
→ If that growth runs on fossil fuels, it could take up 44% of the power-sector emissions the US can afford under its 2035 climate target. On renewables, the same growth would take 4%.
→ At least 15 US coal plants scheduled to retire stayed open in 2025, amid rising demand and federal emergency orders.
→ Natural gas already supplies over 40% of US data-centre electricity.
→ Operational emissions at Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Alphabet rose 138–182% between 2020 and 2023.

The technology isn't fixed. The energy behind it is a choice that procurement, policy and disclosure can still shape.

## LinkedIn

AI's energy use has stopped being a footnote in climate planning.

A 60-second explainer, with every figure sourced on screen:

→ US data centres used ~183 TWh in 2024, about 4% of national electricity. Projections for 2030 range from 9% to 12%.
→ If that growth runs on fossil fuels, it could account for 44% of the power-sector emissions the US can afford under its 2035 target. On renewables, the same growth accounts for 4%.
→ At least 15 US coal plants scheduled to retire stayed open in 2025, amid rising demand and federal emergency orders.
→ Natural gas already supplies over 40% of US data-centre electricity, and new turbines operate for decades.
→ Operational emissions at Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Alphabet rose 138–182% between 2020 and 2023.
→ Cooling could require up to 1.1 billion m³ of water a year by 2030.
→ Public-health costs from the power behind AI are projected above $20bn a year by 2030 (preprint).

The difference between 44% and 4% isn't the technology. It's the energy behind it: 24/7 clean power matched to demand, siting where energy and water allow, efficient chips, and disclosure at the site level.

Those are procurement and policy decisions, and most of them are still open.
