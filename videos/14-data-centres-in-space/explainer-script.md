## Full voiceover (~250 words)

> On the first of October 2026, Google launched AI chips into space, to test whether the next data centre should orbit Earth.
>
> Why would anyone do that? Because on Earth, AI data centres are hitting limits. Grid connections take years. Land is contested. Cooling needs water. And neighbours are pushing back.
>
> Space offers one big thing: sunlight. In a special "dawn-to-dusk" orbit, a satellite rides the line between day and night, so its solar panels almost never go dark. Google says a panel there can be up to eight times more productive than the same panel on Earth. And there's no land to buy and no local water to use.
>
> It's already being tested. In November 2025, a startup called Starcloud put a single Nvidia H100 chip in orbit and trained a small AI model on it. Google's prototype carries four of its own AI chips. Its long-term design is a cluster of 81 satellites flying just 100 to 200 metres apart, linked by lasers, working as one data centre. And SpaceX has asked US regulators to approve up to 1 million computing satellites. For scale: about 16,800 working satellites orbit Earth today, all of them combined.
>
> Now the hard part: cooling. On Earth, air or water carries heat away. In space, there's no air, so heat can only leave as infrared light, radiating from big panels. One World Economic Forum estimate puts space-station-style radiators at about 100 tonnes for just one megawatt of computing. Newer designs may be much lighter, but this is the core engineering problem.
>
> Then there's radiation. Charged particles in orbit can flip bits and wear out chips. Google fired a proton beam at its chips on the ground, and they survived a dose bigger than five years in orbit. And there are no repairs up there: a failed chip stays failed. Google itself says this only adds up by the mid-2030s, and only if launch costs keep falling.
>
> And space has costs of its own: rocket emissions, more debris in crowded orbits, and brighter night skies for astronomers.
>
> So orbital data centres are a real experiment, not an escape hatch. The clean power AI needs this decade still has to be built here on the ground. Would you trust your data to a satellite?
