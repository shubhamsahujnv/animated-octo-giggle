## Page copy

### H1: A custom software development process built for speed and certainty

Most software projects don't fail because the engineers can't code. They fail because nobody agreed what "done" meant, the architecture was guessed rather than tested, and security turned up a week before launch.

Our process is designed around those three failure points. It has five stages. Each one ends with something you can see, check and keep, whether or not you carry on with us.

**[Book a discovery call]** **[See engagement models]**

---

### H2: The five stages at a glance

| Stage | What happens | What you walk away with |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Discover** | Goals, constraints, users and success metrics | A scope, a risk register and a **cost envelope** |
| **2. Architect** | Target architecture, security model, delivery plan | Architecture decision records and a **running slice** |
| **3. Build** | Short cycles that ship production increments | Working software every two weeks, in your repo |
| **4. Harden** | Security, performance and reliability testing | Test reports, load results, an observability stack |
| **5. Scale & operate** | Run, observe, optimise, hand over | Runbooks, documentation, trained team, SLA if needed |

---

### H2: Stage 1, Discover: agree what success looks like before anyone writes code

Discovery is short and fixed-price, typically one to two weeks. A senior engineer and a product lead work with your team to pin down:

- **The business goal.** What changes for the business if this works? Revenue, cost, risk or speed.
- **The users and their jobs.** Who uses it, how often, and what they do today instead.
- **The constraints.** Budget, deadlines, regulation, existing systems, data you can and can't move.
- **The success metrics.** Two or three numbers we'll both be held to.

You leave discovery with a written scope, a ranked risk register and a **cost envelope**: the number for what the build will cost and how long it will take, with the assumptions written down, so you can tell when something changes. Not a range with a disclaimer. You're not committed to anything else. If you want to take the scope to another firm, it's yours.

[Read more about discovery →](/process/discover)

---

### H2: Stage 2, Architect: prove the design with a running slice

Diagrams don't fail in production. Systems do. So we don't stop at a diagram.

During architecture we design the target system: services, data model, integrations, hosting, identity and the security model. Every significant choice gets an **architecture decision record (ADR)**, a short document that says what we chose, what we rejected and why. Six months later, when someone asks "why Postgres and not DynamoDB?", the answer is in the repo.

Then we build a **running slice**: one thin path through the real system, deployed to real infrastructure. It proves the riskiest assumptions, such as latency, an awkward integration or a data-volume question, before the budget is committed to them.

[Read more about architecture →](/process/architect)

---

### H2: Stage 3, Build: production increments from week one

We work in two-week increments. Each one ends with working software deployed to a staging or production environment, not a slide that says "60% complete".

- **You own the code from day one.** It lives in your GitHub or GitLab organisation, not ours.
- **Trunk-based, reviewed, with CI from the first commit.** Nothing merges without review and a green pipeline, and you have commit access throughout.
- **You see progress in software, not status reports.** A demo every two weeks, a written update every week.
- **Scope changes are visible.** If something new comes in, we show you what it displaces or what it adds to the envelope.

[Read more about the build stage →](/process/build)

---

### H2: Stage 4, Harden: security and reliability built in, not bolted on

Hardening isn't a phase we start at the end. Security scanning, dependency checks and performance tests run in the pipeline from the start. Before launch we add the checks that need a near-finished system:

- Threat modelling and a security review against the risks we logged in discovery
- Load and soak testing at realistic and peak traffic
- Failure testing: what happens when a dependency is slow or down
- Observability: logs, metrics, traces and alerts wired to the people who'll respond

[Read more about hardening →](/process/harden)

---

### H2: Stage 5, Scale & operate: run it, improve it, hand it over

After launch we can run the system for you under an SLA, train your team to run it, or both. Either way the goal is that you are never dependent on us.

Handover includes runbooks, architecture documentation, knowledge-transfer sessions, paired on-call shifts and a defined support taper. All code, infrastructure-as-code and credentials transfer to you. There is no licence-back, no retained components and no hosting lock-in.

[Read more about scale and operate →](/process/scale-and-operate)

---

### H2: How this differs from a textbook SDLC

If you've read about the software development life cycle, the stages will look familiar. The differences are in what we refuse to skip:

| Common practice | What we do instead |
|---|---|
| Estimate after a sales call | Estimate after a paid discovery, with assumptions written down |
| Architecture as a diagram | Architecture as ADRs plus a running slice |
| Code in the vendor's repo until final payment | Code in your repo from the first commit |

### H2: Why we insist on discovery

Most estimates are made after a single sales call, with half the facts. Either they're padded to cover the unknowns or they're optimistic and then blow out. A short, paid discovery replaces guesswork with written assumptions. It's the cheapest risk reduction in the whole project.

---

### H2: What happens in discovery

| Week | Activities |
|---|---|
| **Week 1: Understand** | Stakeholder interviews, current-state review, user and workflow mapping, existing systems and data audit |
| **Week 2: Shape and commit** | Scope options, key user journeys, technical risk spikes, integration and data checks, early architecture sketch |
| **End of week 2** | Scope agreed, risk register ranked, cost envelope and timeline, delivery plan, readout session |

Who's involved from Pixorama: a senior engineer who will lead the build, plus a product lead. Specialists in AI, security or cloud join where the problem needs them.

Who we need from you: a decision-maker, a product owner, and access to anyone who knows the current systems.

For AI projects, discovery also produces an **evaluation harness**: real questions with known-good answers, scored automatically on every change, built before the first feature.

---

### H2: What you walk away with

1. **A written scope:** what's in, what's out, and the success metrics we'll both be held to.
2. **A ranked risk register:** technical, data, integration, regulatory and delivery risks, each with a mitigation.
3. **A cost envelope:** the cost and timeline, with every assumption written down. If an assumption changes later, everyone can see what changed and why.
4. **A delivery plan:** milestones, team shape and the first increments.
5. **An initial architecture direction**, refined in the [architect stage](/process/architect).

All of it is yours. If you take it to another firm or your in-house team, that's fine.

---

### H2: What is a cost envelope?

A cost envelope is the number for what a build will cost and how long it will take, with every assumption behind it written down. For example: "this assumes the existing customer API can be used as is."

It's not a range with a disclaimer. Because the assumptions are written down, both sides can see exactly when something has changed, and what that change does to the cost.

Want a ballpark before discovery? Try our [software development cost calculator](/resources/software-development-cost-calculator).

- You have an idea or a business case, but no technical scope.

### Calculations

```
missed_calls          = calls × missed_rate
lost_callers          = missed_calls × no_callback_rate
lost_revenue_month    = lost_callers × conversion_rate × customer_value

recovered_revenue     = lost_revenue_month × ai_handle_rate
    // AI answers every call; assume it can convert only the share of calls it can fully handle

staff_minutes_saved   = calls × (1 − missed_rate) × ai_handle_rate × call_length
staff_cost_saved      = (staff_minutes_saved / 60) × staff_hourly_cost

ai_minutes            = calls × call_length        // AI answers all calls; escalated calls still use AI minutes
ai_cost_month         = ai_minutes × ai_cost_per_min + platform_fee

net_benefit_month     = recovered_revenue + staff_cost_saved − ai_cost_month
roi_percent           = net_benefit_month / ai_cost_month × 100
break_even_calls      = ai_cost_month / (conversion_rate × customer_value)
                        // recovered bookings needed per month to cover the AI cost
```

### Outputs (big numbers, top to bottom)

1. **Revenue lost to missed calls each month:** `lost_revenue_month`
2. **Revenue an AI agent could recover:** `recovered_revenue`
3. **Staff time saved:** `staff_minutes_saved / 60` hours (`staff_cost_saved`)
4. **AI agent cost:** `ai_cost_month`
5. **Net monthly benefit:** `net_benefit_month` (green if positive, neutral if negative)
6. **Break-even:** "You need **N** recovered bookings a month to cover the cost."

### H2: How the calculation works

The calculator compares two costs:

**The cost of doing nothing.** Every missed call has a chance of being a customer. If they don't call back, that revenue is lost. We multiply your missed calls by the share who don't call back, your conversion rate and your customer value.

**The cost of an AI agent.** Per-minute charges for every call the agent answers, plus any monthly platform fee.

The benefit is the revenue the agent recovers, plus the staff time it frees up, minus what it costs.

We've deliberately kept the assumptions conservative: the AI only "recovers" calls it can fully handle, and it's charged for every call it answers, including ones it hands to a person.

---

### H2: Where to find your numbers

- **Calls per month and missed-call rate:** your phone system or VoIP provider's call reports. Many show "unanswered" or "abandoned" calls.
- **Conversion rate:** bookings ÷ answered enquiry calls. If you don't track it, ask whoever answers the phone for a rough estimate.
- **Customer value:** use the first-booking value to be conservative, or lifetime value if you know it.
- **AI cost:** get an all-in per-minute price, including speech, model and telephony, from any provider you're considering. See [how to compare AI voice agent quotes](/products/pxo-ai/pricing).

- **Customer experience:** some callers prefer a person, and some prefer a fast answer at 9pm.

- **Seasonality:** run it with your peak-month numbers too.
- **Upside beyond recovery:** after-hours bookings, reminder calls that reduce no-shows, and call data you can learn from.

## Page copy

### H1: What is an AI voice agent?

> **An AI voice agent is software that holds a natural spoken conversation, usually over the phone. It listens to what a caller says, works out what they want, replies in a human-sounding voice, and can take actions such as booking an appointment, looking up an order or transferring the call to a person.**

Unlike a traditional phone menu ("press 1 for sales"), an AI voice agent understands open-ended speech and responds to what the caller actually said.

---

### H2: How an AI voice agent works

Every turn of the conversation runs through the same loop, usually in under a second:

1. **Listen: speech-to-text (STT).** The caller's audio is transcribed into text as they speak.
2. **Understand and decide: a large language model (LLM).** The model reads the transcript and conversation history and decides what to do: answer, ask a clarifying question, or take an action.
3. **Look things up: retrieval (RAG).** For factual answers, the agent searches the business's own knowledge, such as FAQs, policies and product data, so the answer is grounded rather than invented. [What is RAG? →](/blog/what-is-rag-in-ai)
4. **Act: tools and integrations.** The agent calls a calendar, CRM or order system to book, check or update something.
5. **Speak: text-to-speech (TTS).** The reply is turned into natural speech and played to the caller.

Around that loop sit **guardrails** (rules on what the agent may and may not say or do), **hand-off rules** (when to transfer to a person) and **logging** (a record of every call for review).

|---|---|---|---|

### H2: What is an AI receptionist?

An **AI receptionist** is an AI voice agent set up to do a receptionist's phone job: greeting callers, answering common questions, booking appointments, taking messages and routing calls. It's the most common use of AI voice agents in small businesses.

### H2: Where AI voice agents fall short

AI voice agents are good at routine, well-defined calls. They're weaker at:

- **Complex, emotional or high-stakes conversations.** These should go to a person.
- **Very noisy lines and strong accents in unsupported languages.** Speech recognition still makes mistakes.
- **Questions not covered by the knowledge base.** A good agent says "I don't know" and takes a message, while a bad one makes something up.
- **Situations needing judgement or authority**, such as negotiating, making exceptions or giving professional advice.

The best deployments use AI for the routine majority and hand everything else to people, with context.

---

### H2: What to look for when choosing one

1. **Latency.** Does it respond quickly enough to feel natural?
2. **Grounding.** Does it answer from your content, and admit when it doesn't know?
3. **Integrations.** Can it actually book, look up and update in your systems?
4. **Hand-off.** Can callers always reach a person, with context passed along?
5. **Visibility.** Can you see every call's transcript, sources and actions?
6. **Compliance.** Does it disclose that it's AI? Does it support consent and calling rules for outbound?
7. **All-in pricing.** Is the per-minute price all-in? [How to compare quotes →](/products/pxo-ai/pricing)

### H2: Frequently asked questions

**Are AI voice agents legal?**
Generally yes for inbound calls. Outbound calls using AI voices are regulated in many countries. In the US, for example, the FCC treats AI-generated voices as "artificial" under the TCPA, so consent rules apply. [Outbound compliance →](/products/pxo-ai/use-cases/outbound)

**Do callers know they're talking to AI?**
They should. Good practice, and in some places the law, is for the agent to say it's an AI at the start of the call.

**How much does an AI voice agent cost?**
Most are priced per minute, sometimes with a monthly platform fee. [See how pricing works](/products/pxo-ai/pricing) or [estimate your ROI](/resources/ai-voice-agent-roi-calculator).

**Can an AI voice agent replace a receptionist?**
It can take over most routine calls. Many businesses use it alongside staff, for overflow, after hours and peak times.

**What's the difference between an AI voice agent and a chatbot?**
A chatbot works in text. A voice agent works in speech, which adds speech recognition, voice synthesis and much tighter latency requirements. Many platforms, including PXO AI, run the same agent on both.

### H2: AI and voice agents {#ai}

**AI agent**
Software that uses an AI model to decide what to do next, such as answering, looking something up or taking an action, in pursuit of a goal, rather than following a fixed script. [How to build one →](/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-agent)

**AI voice agent**
An AI agent that holds spoken phone conversations: it listens, understands, replies in natural speech and can take actions like booking appointments. [Full definition →](/resources/glossary/ai-voice-agent)

**AI receptionist**
An AI voice agent set up to answer a business's phone line: greeting callers, answering common questions, booking appointments and taking messages. [AI receptionist vs answering service →](/blog/ai-answering-service-vs-human-receptionist)

**Conversational AI**
Technology that lets software hold a natural conversation in text or speech. [Buyer's guide →](/blog/conversational-ai-platform-buyers-guide)

**Large language model (LLM)**
An AI model trained on large amounts of text that can understand and generate language. LLMs power most modern chat and voice agents.

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**
A technique where an AI system looks up relevant information from your own documents before answering, so responses are grounded in your content rather than the model's memory. [What is RAG? →](/blog/what-is-rag-in-ai)

**Fine-tuning**
Further training an existing AI model on your own examples to change its behaviour or style. [RAG vs fine-tuning →](/blog/rag-vs-fine-tuning)

**Embedding**
A list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text, so a computer can find text with similar meaning. Embeddings make RAG search work.

**Vector database**
A database designed to store embeddings and quickly find the closest matches to a query.

**Guardrails**
Rules applied to an AI system's inputs and outputs to keep it within safe, approved behaviour, for example blocking certain topics or requiring a disclosure.

**Hallucination**
When an AI model states something false with confidence. Retrieval, guardrails and evaluation reduce it.

**Evaluation (evals)**
A repeatable set of test cases used to measure an AI system's quality, so you can tell whether a change made it better or worse.

**Prompt injection**
An attack where text supplied to an AI system tries to override its instructions, for example a document that says "ignore your rules."

**Speech-to-text (STT) / text-to-speech (TTS)**
STT turns spoken audio into text, and TTS turns text into natural-sounding speech. A voice agent uses both on every turn.

**Latency (in voice AI)**
The delay between a caller finishing speaking and the agent starting to reply. Long latency is what makes voice bots feel robotic.

**Warm transfer**
Handing a call from an AI agent to a person along with a summary of the conversation, so the caller doesn't have to repeat themselves.

**Containment rate**
The share of calls or chats an AI agent resolves without handing over to a person.

**White-label AI**
An AI product that a reseller can sell under their own brand. [PXO AI white label →](/products/pxo-ai/white-label)

### H1: Build vs buy AI agents: how to decide

Every team putting AI agents into production hits the same question: build our own on top of models and APIs, or buy a platform that already does most of it? The right answer depends less on technology than on how central the agent is to your business, and how much ongoing work you're prepared to own.

*Full disclosure: we do both. We build custom AI agents for clients and we build our own platform, [PXO AI](/products/pxo-ai). We'll tell you which one we'd pick in your situation.*

---

### H2: The short answer

- **Buy** when the agent does a common job (answering phones, booking, tier-1 support), speed matters, and the agent isn't what makes you different.
- **Build** when the agent *is* the product or a core differentiator, you have unusual requirements a platform can't meet, or you have strict data and hosting constraints.
- **Buy, then extend** is the most common right answer: start on a platform, and build custom integrations and logic around it.

---

### H2: What "building" actually involves

Teams underestimate building because the first demo is easy. A working prototype can take a day. A production agent needs all of this:

| Layer | What you'd own |
|---|---|
| **Models** | Choosing, routing and upgrading LLMs; handling provider outages |
| **Speech** (for voice) | Speech-to-text, text-to-speech, turn-taking, interruptions, latency tuning |
| **Telephony** (for voice) | Numbers, SIP, transfers, recording, regional rules |
| **Retrieval** | Ingestion, chunking, embeddings, vector store, freshness, permissions ([what is RAG?](/blog/what-is-rag-in-ai)) |
| **Tools and integrations** | Every system the agent acts on, plus auth, retries and errors |
| **Guardrails** | Topic limits, disclosures, PII handling, prompt-injection defence |
| **Evaluation** | Test sets, regression runs on every change, quality dashboards |
| **Observability** | Traces, transcripts, cost per conversation, alerting |
| **Operations** | On-call, incident response, model-version changes, cost control |

Our step-by-step guide covers the build path in detail: [how to build an AI agent](/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-agent).

---

### H2: What "buying" actually gets you, and what it doesn't

**You get:** most of the layers above, already built and maintained; faster launch; someone else on call for the platform.

**You don't get:**
- Unlimited flexibility, because you're bound by the platform's model choices, integrations and roadmap
- Ownership of the platform. Check data export and what happens if you leave.
- A free lunch on integration work, since connecting to *your* systems is still work
- Freedom from evaluation. You still need to test the agent on your own calls and content.

---

### H2: The decision scorecard

Score each question from 1 (strongly buy) to 5 (strongly build):

| Question | 1 = Buy | 5 = Build |
|---|---|---|
| Is the agent your core product or a differentiator? | Supporting function | It *is* the product |
| How standard is the job? | Common (reception, booking, FAQs) | Unusual workflow or domain |
| Data and hosting constraints? | Standard cloud is fine | Must run in your VPC or region, strict residency |
| Engineering capacity for ongoing AI ops? | None or little | A team that can own it long-term |
| Time to first launch? | Weeks | Months is acceptable |
| Volume and unit economics? | Low to moderate | Very high volume, where per-minute margins matter |
| Need to control models and costs directly? | No | Yes |

**7 to 15:** buy. **16 to 24:** buy, then extend. **25 to 35:** build.

### H2: Cost: the comparison people get wrong

**Buying** looks expensive per minute and cheap to start. **Building** looks cheap per minute (raw API costs) and is expensive to start and to run.

What a fair comparison includes:

| | Buy | Build |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront | Setup, integration | Engineering build (often months of a small team) |
| Per conversation | Platform per-minute rate | Raw model + speech + telephony costs |
| Ongoing people | Admin, content updates | Engineers for evaluation, ops and upgrades |
| Risk | Vendor dependency | Delivery and maintenance risk |

At low to moderate volumes, buying is almost always cheaper in total. At very high volumes, building can win on unit cost, if you have the team to run it.

### H2: Frequently asked questions

**What's the difference between staff augmentation and managed services?**
With staff augmentation you manage the engineers and own the outcome. With managed services the provider runs a defined function for you under an SLA and owns the outcome.

**Is staff augmentation the same as outsourcing?**
It's one form of it. "Outsourcing" covers any work handed to an outside firm. Staff augmentation is the version where you keep control and the engineers work inside your team.

**Is a dedicated development team the same as staff augmentation?**
No. A dedicated team comes with its own leadership and owns delivery. With augmentation, the engineers report to you.

**Which is best for a startup?**
Startups without a technical leader usually do better with a project team or a [fractional CTO](/engagement-models/advisory-architecture) plus a small squad. Startups with a strong CTO often augment.
