# 9 Common Carbon Accounting Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

A carbon footprint is only useful if people trust it. As sustainability claims face more scrutiny from regulators, investors and the public, small errors in your emissions inventory can turn into big credibility problems, or even greenwashing accusations.

Here are the nine mistakes we see most often, and how to avoid each one.

---

## 1. Ignoring Scope 3 because it's "too hard"

**The mistake:** Reporting only Scope 1 and 2 and implying that's the full footprint.

**Why it matters:** For most businesses, Scope 3 is the largest share of emissions. Leaving it out gives a misleading picture and hides your biggest reduction opportunities.

**The fix:** Screen all 15 Scope 3 categories, estimate the significant ones (spend-based estimates are fine to start) and improve data quality each year.

## 2. Double counting emissions

**The mistake:** Counting the same emissions twice, for example including company-owned vehicle fuel in Scope 1 *and* in business travel under Scope 3.

**The fix:** Define clear organisational and operational boundaries, and map each data source to exactly one scope and category.

## 3. Forgetting refrigerants

**The mistake:** Leaving out HFC leaks from air conditioning, chillers and cold storage.

**Why it matters:** Many refrigerants have global warming potentials hundreds or thousands of times higher than CO₂. A small leak can outweigh a lot of fuel.

**The fix:** Use maintenance and top-up records to estimate leakage, and include refrigerants in Scope 1.

## 4. Using outdated or mismatched emission factors

**The mistake:** Reusing the same factors year after year, or using another country's grid factor.

**The fix:** Use current, geography-specific factors, record their sources and versions, and review them every year.

## 5. Reporting only one Scope 2 method

**The mistake:** Showing only market-based Scope 2 after buying renewable energy certificates, which can look like emissions vanished.

**The fix:** Report both **location-based** and **market-based** Scope 2, as the GHG Protocol recommends, and be open about the instruments you use.

## 6. No clear base year or recalculation policy

**The mistake:** Comparing this year's footprint to last year's after an acquisition, a divestment or a method change, without adjusting the baseline.

**The fix:** Set a base year, write a recalculation policy and restate the baseline when significant changes happen. Then your progress claims are like-for-like.

## 7. Weak documentation and audit trails

**The mistake:** Numbers in the final report can't be traced back to invoices, meter readings or calculations.

**Why it matters:** Auditors, verifiers and customers will ask. If you can't show your working, your numbers won't hold up.

**The fix:** Keep a traceable record for every data point: source, date, emission factor and calculation. Technology like blockchain-backed records can make this tamper-evident.

## 8. Treating carbon credits as a substitute for reductions

**The mistake:** Claiming "carbon neutral" or "net zero" mainly by buying offsets while gross emissions stay flat or grow.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the biggest greenwashing risks. Credits don't remove emissions from your inventory.

**The fix:** Put real reductions first. Use high-quality credits only for residual emissions, as part of a wider decarbonization strategy, and report them separately from your gross inventory.

## 9. Measuring once a year and then forgetting about it

**The mistake:** Treating carbon accounting as a once-a-year compliance exercise.

**The fix:** Track emissions continuously so you can see the impact of efficiency projects, spot anomalies early and keep reduction plans on course.

## Quick checklist before you publish your footprint

- [ ] All relevant Scope 3 categories screened
- [ ] Every source mapped to one scope only
- [ ] Refrigerants included
- [ ] Emission factors current and documented
- [ ] Location- and market-based Scope 2 reported
- [ ] Base year and recalculation policy defined
- [ ] Full audit trail available
- [ ] Carbon credits reported separately from gross emissions

### FAQ

**What is greenwashing in carbon reporting?**
Greenwashing means making environmental claims that are misleading or unsupported, such as overstating reductions or hiding major emission sources. Accurate, transparent carbon accounting is the best defence.

**How can I make my carbon data audit-ready?**
Follow a recognised standard like the GHG Protocol or ISO 14064, document every assumption and keep a traceable link from source data to final figures.
