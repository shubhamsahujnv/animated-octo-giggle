Quick answer: Common Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions examples are boiler fuel, company vehicles and refrigerant leaks (Scope 1); purchased electricity, steam, heat and cooling (Scope 2); and purchased goods, freight, travel and product use (Scope 3). Scope 3 spans 15 GHG Protocol categories [1], and in CDP data it averaged 26 times companies' operational emissions [2].

Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions are the three groups the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol uses to sort a company's greenhouse gases by how directly the company controls them. Every emission source in your business belongs in exactly one scope. Getting the classification right is the difference between a credible inventory and one that double counts or misses whole categories.

Below are the top 15 Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions examples, grouped by scope, plus the full list of Scope 3 categories.

What is the main difference between Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions?

The main difference is control: Scope 1 is emitted directly by sources you own or control, Scope 2 is emitted at power plants to make the energy you buy, and Scope 3 is emitted by other companies in your value chain. Scope 3 alone is split into 15 categories, 8 upstream and 7 downstream [1].

Scope 1 Scope 2 Scope 3
What it covers Direct emissions from owned or controlled sources Purchased electricity, steam, heat and cooling All other indirect value-chain emissions
Where it happens On your sites and in your vehicles At the generator or utility At suppliers, carriers, customers and waste handlers
Your control High Medium (you choose suppliers and contracts) Influence, not control
Typical data Fuel logs, refrigerant top-ups Utility bills, energy contracts Supplier data, spend, freight, travel records
2026 reporting example California SB 253, first report was due 10 Nov 2026 Same SB 253 from 2027 [3]

A simple test: if the smokestack, tailpipe or leaking valve is yours, it's Scope 1. If you bought the energy, it's Scope 2. If someone else emitted it on your behalf, it's Scope 3.

What are Scope 1 emissions examples?

Scope 1 emissions examples include fuel burned in company vehicles, boilers and generators, gases released by industrial processes, and refrigerant leaks. These 5 sources cover most Scope 1 inventories.

1. Company-owned or controlled vehicles

Diesel and petrol burned in delivery vans, trucks, forklifts and sales cars that you own or lease and operate are Scope 1. The US EPA's emission factor for diesel is 10.21 kg of CO₂ per gallon [4], so a fleet using 10,000 gallons a year emits about 102 tonnes of CO₂.

2. Boilers, furnaces and kilns

Natural gas, LPG, coal or oil burned on site for heat or steam is Scope 1. EPA lists natural gas at 53.06 kg of CO₂ per million Btu [4]. For many factories this is the largest single Scope 1 line.

3. Backup generators

Diesel generators that run during power cuts count, even if they run only a few hundred hours a year. They are easy to forget because their fuel is often bought in bulk and booked as a general expense.

4. Refrigerant and other fugitive leaks

Leaks from air conditioners, chillers and cold rooms are Scope 1. HFC-134a has a 100-year GWP of 1,530 under the IPCC's AR6 values [5], so a 10 kg leak equals 15.3 tonnes of CO₂e. Use maintenance and top-up records to estimate how much leaked.

5. Process emissions

Chemical reactions that release greenhouse gases directly are Scope 1, separate from fuel use. Common examples include cement clinker production, aluminium smelting and chemical processes such as nitric acid production, where nitrous oxide abatement now has a UN Article 6.4 methodology.

Is electricity Scope 1 or Scope 2?

Purchased electricity is Scope 2, while electricity you generate yourself from fuel you burn shows up as Scope 1 through that fuel. The GHG Protocol requires companies with contractual instruments, such as renewable certificates, to report Scope 2 both location-based and market-based [6].

6. Grid electricity for offices, plants and warehouses

Every kWh bought from the grid is Scope 2. The US average grid emitted 823.1 lb of CO₂ per MWh in 2022, according to EPA's eGRID [7], or about 0.37 tonnes per MWh. Indian companies typically use the Central Electricity Authority's grid factors instead, one of several differences between Indian and US reporting.

7. Purchased steam or heat

Steam or hot water bought from a neighbouring plant or a district heating network is Scope 2. This is common in industrial parks where one utility serves several factories.

8. Purchased cooling

Chilled water bought from a district cooling network is Scope 2. The emissions happen at the cooling plant, but your consumption drives them.

What are Scope 3 emissions examples?

Scope 3 emissions examples include purchased materials, freight, business travel, commuting, waste, and the use and disposal of the products you sell. In 2023 CDP disclosures, supply-chain emissions averaged 26 times operational emissions, and 92 times in retail [2].

9. Purchased goods and services (category 1)

The emissions from producing everything you buy, from steel and plastics to software and cleaning services, fall here. For manufacturers and retailers this is often the largest Scope 3 category.

10. Upstream transportation and distribution (category 4)

Third-party freight that brings materials to your sites, and some outbound freight you pay for, is category 4. Fuel burned in your own trucks stays in Scope 1.

11. Business travel (category 6)

Flights, trains, taxis and hotel stays for work trips are category 6. Employee-owned cars used for business also land here, not in Scope 1.

12. Employee commuting (category 7)

The daily commute of your staff, in their own vehicles or on public transport, is category 7.

13. Waste generated in operations (category 5)

Landfill, incineration, recycling and wastewater treatment of the waste your sites produce are category 5. Emissions happen at the waste company, so they are indirect.

14. Use of sold products (category 11)

Energy your products consume during their life, such as electricity used by appliances or fuel burned by vehicles, is category 11. For makers of machinery or vehicles it can dwarf every other scope.

15. End-of-life treatment of sold products (category 12)

What happens to your products and packaging after customers throw them away is category 12. Landfill, incineration and recycling each carry different emissions.

What are the 15 Scope 3 categories?

The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 Standard defines 15 categories: 8 upstream, tied to what you buy, and 7 downstream, tied to what you sell [1]. The categories are designed to be mutually exclusive, so one emission never sits in two categories of the same inventory.

# Category Direction
1 Purchased goods and services Upstream
2 Capital goods Upstream
3 Fuel- and energy-related activities (not in Scope 1 or 2) Upstream

7 Employee commuting Upstream

10 Processing of sold products Downstream

14 Franchises Downstream

Not every category applies to every company [1]. Screen all 15, document why any are excluded, and put your data effort into the largest ones.

How do you calculate Scope 3 emissions?

You calculate Scope 3 emissions by multiplying activity or spend data by an emission factor for each category. For purchased goods, the GHG Protocol describes 4 methods, from most to least specific: supplier-specific, hybrid, average-data and spend-based [8].

  • Supplier-specific: product-level cradle-to-gate data collected from your suppliers
  • Hybrid: supplier data where you have it, secondary data to fill the gaps
  • Average-data: physical quantities (tonnes of steel, kg of plastic) times industry-average factors
  • Spend-based: money spent times average emissions per unit of currency

Companies can mix methods within one category, choosing by data availability and quality [8]. Most start spend-based to find the hotspots, then move the biggest suppliers to supplier-specific data year by year. For a practical sequence, see Scope 3 for manufacturers without a data team.

What happens after you classify your emissions by scope?

After classifying every source, the next 3 steps are to set a base year, calculate totals with documented emission factors, and rank the largest sources for reduction. Classification is the map; the inventory and the reduction plan are the journey.

Common next moves:

  1. Lock a base year you'll measure progress against.
  2. Report Scope 2 both ways if you buy renewable energy or certificates.
  3. Prioritise Scope 3 categories by size and by how much influence you have.
  4. Engage suppliers behind your largest purchased-goods emissions.

If you'd rather not run this in spreadsheets, a Scope 1, 2 and 3 carbon accounting platform can hold the inventory, factors and evidence in one place. You can also ask a carbon accounting specialist which categories matter most for your sector, or read more practical carbon accounting field notes.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What are Scope 3 emissions for a bank?+

A bank reports its lending and investment portfolio under Scope 3 category 15, investments, often called financed emissions: the emissions of the companies and projects it lends to or invests in [1]. For most banks this category matters far more than offices, branches and travel, because the portfolio is where the money and the emissions sit.

Are company car emissions Scope 1 or Scope 3?+

It depends on who owns or controls the car. Vehicles the company owns or leases and operates are Scope 1. An employee's personal car used for a work trip is Scope 3 category 6, business travel, and the same car used to drive to work is category 7, employee commuting [1].

Does buying renewable electricity make Scope 2 zero?+

Only in the market-based figure. Renewable contracts and certificates can reduce market-based Scope 2, sometimes to zero, but the location-based figure still reflects the average grid mix. That is why the GHG Protocol requires companies with such instruments to report both totals side by side [6].

Is Scope 3 reporting mandatory in 2026?+

For most companies it is still voluntary in 2026, but that is changing. California's SB 253 requires Scope 1 and 2 reports from large companies in 2026 and adds Scope 3 from 2027 [3]. Large customers also ask suppliers for data, which makes Scope 3 a commercial requirement even where law does not.

Doesn't Scope 3 double count emissions between companies?+

Yes, across companies, and that's by design. Your Scope 1 is part of your customer's Scope 3, so totals across a supply chain overlap. Within one company's inventory, though, the 15 categories are mutually exclusive, so an emission must never be counted twice [1].

Is water use Scope 1, 2 or 3?+

Water itself is not a greenhouse gas, but supplying and treating it uses energy. Water bought from a utility is usually reported in Scope 3 category 1, purchased goods and services. Wastewater sent off site for treatment falls under category 5, waste generated in operations [1].
