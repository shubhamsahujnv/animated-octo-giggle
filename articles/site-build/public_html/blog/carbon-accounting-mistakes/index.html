Top 9 Carbon Accounting Mistakes to Avoid in 2026 and How to Fix Them

A kWh-for-MWh slip is a 1,000x error. The top 9 carbon accounting mistakes companies make in 2026, from double counting to offset claims, and the fixes.

Quick answer: The top 9 carbon accounting mistakes in 2026 are leaving out Scope 3, double counting, reporting only market-based Scope 2, using stale emission factors, missing refrigerant leaks, unit errors, skipping base-year recalculation, counting carbon credits as reductions, and missing reporting deadlines such as California's 10 November 2026 SB 253 date [1]. Each one can be fixed before you publish.

Carbon accounting mistakes are errors in how a company measures, classifies or reports its greenhouse gas emissions. In 2026 they cost more than they used to. Regulators in California, India and the EU now set firm dates, and since 27 September 2026 EU rules restrict product climate claims based on offsetting [2].

Below are the top 9 mistakes, why each one happens, and how to fix it.

Why is carbon accounting so error-prone?

Carbon accounting is error-prone because data comes from dozens of systems in different units, and most of the footprint sits outside your own walls. CDP found companies are twice as likely to measure Scope 1 and 2 as Scope 3 [3], even though supply-chain emissions average 26 times operational ones.

Three structural reasons stand out:

  • Fragmented data: fuel, power, travel and purchasing records live with different teams
  • Judgment calls: boundaries, factors and methods all involve choices that must be documented
  • Manual work: spreadsheets make transcription and unit errors easy and hard to spot

None of this means your numbers are wrong. It means they need controls, the same way financial accounts do.

What are the top 9 carbon accounting mistakes?

The 9 mistakes below cover completeness (1 and 2), method (3 to 7) and claims and compliance (8 and 9). Mistakes 1 and 8 carry the biggest reputational risk, and mistake 6 the biggest numerical risk.

1. Leaving out Scope 3 because it's hard

Reporting Scope 1 and 2 alone, and implying that's the whole footprint, hides the largest share. In 2023 CDP disclosures, supply-chain emissions averaged 26 times operational emissions [3]. Fix: screen all 15 Scope 3 categories, estimate the significant ones (spend-based is fine to start), and start with the categories that carry the weight.

2. Double counting inside your own inventory

The GHG Protocol designs the 15 Scope 3 categories to be mutually exclusive within one company's inventory [4]. A common slip is counting fuel for leased trucks in Scope 1 and again as category 4 freight. Fix: map every data source to exactly one scope and category, and keep that map in the inventory file.

3. Reporting only market-based Scope 2

Showing only the market-based figure after buying renewable certificates can make emissions look as if they vanished. The GHG Protocol's Scope 2 Guidance requires both location-based and market-based totals for companies with contractual instruments [5]. Fix: report both, side by side, and name the instruments behind the market-based number.

4. Using stale or wrong-country emission factors

Reusing year-one factors, or applying another country's grid factor, quietly skews every total. National databases update regularly: the US EPA's eGRID reached its eighteenth edition in January 2025 [6]. Fix: review factors every year, match them to geography and year, and record source and version for each.

5. Forgetting refrigerant leaks

Leaks from chillers and air conditioners are Scope 1, and they're potent. HFC-134a has an AR6 global warming potential of 1,530 [7], so a 10 kg leak equals 15.3 tonnes of CO₂e. Fix: pull top-up quantities from maintenance logs every quarter and treat them as leakage unless proven otherwise.

6. Unit and transcription errors

Mixing kWh with MWh is a 1,000× error; gallons versus litres is nearly 4×. These slips usually happen when invoice figures are typed into spreadsheets. Fix: import data directly where possible, add range checks that flag year-on-year jumps above a set percentage, and have a second person review the largest sources.

7. Not recalculating the base year

Comparing this year to a base year that still includes a sold plant overstates your progress. The SBTi's Corporate Net-Zero Standard V2.0 says companies shall recalculate their base-year inventory after significant changes [8]. Fix: write a recalculation policy with a clear significance threshold and apply it after every acquisition, divestment or method change.

8. Treating carbon credits as reductions

Credits never change the emissions in your inventory. Since 27 September 2026, EU Directive 2024/825 bans product claims of neutral, reduced or positive climate impact that rely on offsetting [2]. Fix: report gross emissions first, report credits separately, and base public claims on real reductions, knowing that buyers now set a higher quality bar for removals.

9. Missing a 2026 reporting deadline

Deadlines arrived fast in 2026. California's first SB 253 Scope 1 and 2 reports were due 10 November 2026 for US-formed companies with over $1 billion revenue doing business in the state [1], and India's BRSR Core reaches the top 1,000 listed companies in FY 2026-27 [9]. Fix: keep a compliance calendar tied to the inventory schedule, and check how Indian and US reporting differ if you operate in both.

Which emission factors should you use in 2026?

In 2026, use the newest emission factors published for your country, year and activity, and record each one's source and version. For US electricity that usually means EPA's eGRID; for Indian grid power, the Central Electricity Authority's CO₂ Baseline Database, which reached version 22.0 [10].

Two rules prevent most factor mistakes: never mix factor years within one reporting year, and never let a factor's age go unrecorded.

What are greenwashing examples in carbon reporting in 2026?

Greenwashing in carbon reporting means making climate claims your data cannot support. The clearest 2026 example is labelling a product "carbon neutral" because of offsets, which EU rules have banned since 27 September 2026 [2].

Common patterns to avoid:

  • Offset-based neutrality claims for products, such as "CO₂ neutral" or "climate compensated" [2]
  • Selective scope reporting that omits Scope 3 while implying full coverage
  • Market-based-only Scope 2 that hides a dirty grid mix
  • Unadjusted base years that make divestments look like reductions

The EU rule has one carve-out: claims based on the actual life-cycle impact of the product within its value chain are still allowed [2]. The safest approach everywhere is to make claims your inventory can prove line by line.

How do you check a GHG inventory report before publishing?

Check a GHG inventory report with a 10-point pre-publication review covering completeness, methods and claims. Most errors that reach print would have been caught by a second reviewer reading against a checklist.

  • All 15 Scope 3 categories screened, exclusions justified
  • Every data source mapped to exactly one scope and category
  • Refrigerant top-ups included in Scope 1
  • Emission factors current, country-specific and versioned
  • Location- and market-based Scope 2 both reported
  • Units checked on the 10 largest sources
  • Base year and recalculation policy stated
  • Year-on-year changes above your threshold explained
  • Carbon credits reported separately from gross emissions
  • Every public claim traceable to inventory data

Keep the completed checklist with the evidence file. Verifiers often ask for it.

What happens after you fix these mistakes?

After fixing these mistakes, the next 2 steps are to restate any affected prior-year figures and to prepare for third-party assurance. California's regulator accepts first-year SB 253 reports with or without assurance for the 2026 cycle [1], so this is the year to build a verifiable trail.

Then put controls in place so the same errors don't return next year:

  1. Automate data intake for the largest sources.
  2. Version your factor library and review it annually.
  3. Separate credits from inventory in every report and dashboard.
  4. Schedule a pre-publication review before every disclosure.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What should you do if you find an error after publishing a GHG report?+

Correct it openly, in 3 steps: restate the affected figures in your next report, explain the cause and the size of the change, and recalculate the base year if the error crosses your significance threshold. For SB 253, California's regulator has said it will use enforcement discretion for the first report due in 2026 [1].

Who verifies a GHG inventory?+

Independent assurance providers, usually accounting firms or accredited verification bodies, check a GHG inventory against a standard such as ISO 14064-3. Requirements are tightening. The SBTi's Corporate Net-Zero Standard V2.0 requires third-party assurance of the target base-year inventory for larger Category A companies from 1 February 2027 [8].

Does California's SB 253 apply to Indian companies?+

Not to a company formed only in India. SB 253 covers entities formed under US law with over $1 billion in annual revenue that do business in California [1]. An Indian group's US-formed subsidiary can be covered if it meets those tests, even though the parent itself is not.

How does the EU CBAM affect carbon accounting for exporters?+

CBAM makes embedded emissions a customs matter. Since 1 January 2026, EU importers of covered goods such as steel, aluminium and cement need authorised declarant status above a 50-tonne threshold, and certificate sales begin on 1 February 2027 [11]. Exporters in India and the US will be asked for per-shipment emissions data.

Is spend-based Scope 3 data a mistake?+

No, as long as you treat it as a starting point. The GHG Protocol lists spend-based as 1 of 4 methods for purchased goods, alongside average-data, hybrid and supplier-specific methods [12]. The mistake is never moving on: shift your largest suppliers to supplier-specific data over time.

Can a company still say "net zero" in 2026?+

Yes, as a target, if it is specific and backed by a plan. The EU offsetting ban targets product claims [2]; claims about future performance, such as net-zero goals, must instead rest on a detailed, verifiable implementation plan. A credible target names a base year, covers all significant scopes and cuts emissions before using removals.
